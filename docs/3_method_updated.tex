\section{The Adaptive Tree of Experts (AToE) PINN method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview of the Method}
\label{sec:overview}

The tree based structure described in
Section~\ref{sec:background} form a natural starting point for a wide range of domain decomposition
designs that combine local models into a Mixture of Experts (MoE). In this
section we present such a method which we refer to as
AToE (Adaptive Tree of Experts). Section~\ref{sec:additional_methods}
describes additional variants of the main AToE algorithm that  can further improve its performance, as illustrated by several examples in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\noindent The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:atoe}, Where each of
its stages is described in detail in the subsections that follow.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{AToE: Adaptive Tree of Experts}
\label{alg:atoe}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require PDE problem, architectures $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{base}}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{exp}}$, budget $M$
\State Train a vanilla PINN $u_0$ with $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{base}}$ to convergence
\State Build a decision tree from $u_0$; trim to an $M$-term complete binary tree
\State Record $u_0$ on the leaf interfaces
\ForAll{leaf regions $\Omega_j$ \textbf{in parallel}}
    \State Train an expert $u_j$ with $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{exp}}$ on $\Omega_j$, with interface data from $u_0$
\EndFor
\State Blend the experts via an adaptive smoothstep POU (partition of unity)
\State Fine tune the blended composition under the global loss
\Ensure Global approximate solution $u_\theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Learning the initial network}
\label{sec:root_training}

The architecture and training process of the initial model $u_0$ follows the classic PINN formulation described in
Section~\ref{sec:background}. However, since its only purpose is to serve as a starting point for the AToE algorithm, we use a relatively small network. For the second order benchmarks we use an MLP
with 5 hidden layers of 64 neurons. For the third and fourth order benchmarks
we use the same architecture combined with a time marching mechanism: the time
axis is split into windows (4 for KdV, 10 for KS), each window is trained in
turn, and the prediction of the converged window at its final time serves as
the initial condition for the next. Training consists of 40k SSBroyden
iterations, with no Adam warm-up. Training uses full batch, with 20k residual
points and 256 points each for the initial and boundary conditions, and all
computations are performed in double precision, as suggested
in~\cite{kiyani2025optimizer}.

\noindent We additionally employ RAD resampling during root training, drawing
half of the residual points from the RAD-weighted distribution and the
remainder uniformly, with the point set refreshed every 500 iterations. RAD is
either helpful or harmless for the root, for instance for problems with a
sharp, localized feature such as the Burgers shock, and keeps the root's
training mechanism consistent with the fine-tuning stage, where RAD
resampling is essential (Section~\ref{sec:smoothstep_windows}).

\vspace{0.5em}

\subsection{Tree Construction and Domain Decomposition}
\label{sec:tree_construction}

The goal of the decomposition is to partition the domain into regions that
reflect the local complexity of the solution. A geometric wavelet tree
$\mathcal{T}$ is constructed from the initial approximation $u_0$ and
compressed using an $M$-term approximation, retaining the $M$ nodes with the
largest geometric wavelet norms $\|\psi_\Omega\|_2$. To preserve a clean
hierarchical structure, both the ancestors and the siblings of the selected nodes are always retained, so that the accepted regions form a complete binary tree.

\noindent When time-marching is employed, the global budget $M$ is distributed
across the windows according to a prescribed allocation strategy (e.g.,
uniform, linear, or quadratic). An independent $M_i$-term tree is then
constructed within each window, with $\sum_i M_i = M$.

\noindent Each leaf region $\Omega_j \in \mathcal{L}$ of the resulting tree is
assigned a neural network expert $u_j$ with the expert architecture
$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{exp}}$, which is smaller than the root architecture. Ee
use an MLP with 3 hidden layers of 20 neurons. Examples of the resulting
decompositions for several benchmark PDEs are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:mterm_trees}.

\subsection{Interpretation of the Adaptive Decomposition}
\label{sec:interpretation}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background}, the equivalence between the
Tree-Besov semi-norm and the geometric wavelet norm implies that the decay of
geometric wavelet coefficients is directly related to the smoothness of the
underlying function.

\noindent A large geometric wavelet norm at a single node does not necessarily
imply that the corresponding region is non-smooth, since nodes near the root
of the tree may simply capture large scale variations of the solution.
Instead, smoothness is reflected in how rapidly the geometric wavelet norms
decay along a branch of the tree. Regions associated with branches whose
coefficients remain significant at increasing depths exhibit slower decay and
therefore correspond to lower regularity and higher local complexity.

\noindent Viewed through this lens, the tree construction procedure described
in Section~\ref{sec:tree_construction} naturally favors the retention of
branches that exhibit slow coefficient decay across multiple levels of the
hierarchy. Consequently, the resulting decomposition concentrates refinement
in the less smooth and more complex portions of the domain, while maintaining
a coarser representation in regions where the coefficient decay is more rapid.

\noindent Extended methods that take further advantage of the theory presented
here are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:additional_methods}. However, even the
simple choice of a fixed expert architecture already applies a form of
adaptive capacity allocation. Regions associated with branches that persist to
greater depths receive a larger number of experts and therefore a larger
effective approximation capacity, whereas smoother regions are represented by
shallower portions of the tree. As illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:capacity_maps}, this mechanism produces a nonlinear
distribution of capacity across the domain that reflects the underlying
complexity of the solution.

\subsection{Expert Training}
\label{sec:expert_training}

Rather than training the experts through the global loss terms, we exploit a
core MoE idea and let each expert solve a localized, and therefore easier,
problem on its own subdomain. Following the interface treatment
of~\cite{luo2025pdd}, each local problem is closed by taking the values and
derivatives on the shared interfaces from the converged root. The predictions of $u_0$ are
recorded on the interior faces of the leaf regions and serve as boundary data
for the local problems. Concretely, for each leaf $j$ we minimize a local
objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_j
= \lambda_r\,\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{res}}_j
+ \lambda_{ic}\,\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ic}}_j
+ \lambda_{bc}\,\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{bc}}_j
+ \lambda_{\Gamma}\,\mathcal{L}^{\Gamma}_j,
\label{eq:leaf_local_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{res}}_j$ enforces the PDE residual on collocation
points inside $\Omega_j$, and $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{ic}}_j$,
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{bc}}_j$ enforce the true initial and boundary conditions
on the parts of $\partial\Omega_j$ that coincide with the physical boundary of
$\Omega$. The region $\Omega_j$ generally borders a different neighbor along
each spatial dimension, so let $\mathcal{I}_j \subseteq \{1,\ldots,d\}$
denote the dimensions along which $\Omega_j$ has an interior neighbor, as
opposed to coinciding with the physical boundary of $\Omega$, and for
$m \in \mathcal{I}_j$ let $X^{\Gamma_j}_m$ denote the sample points on the
corresponding interior face of $\Omega_j$. Let
$x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ denote the spatial coordinates and $N$
the differential order of the PDE. A well posed local problem of order $N$
needs $N$ pieces of interface data, so on the face normal to $x_m$ we match
the value together with derivatives $\partial_{x_m}^k$ up to order $N-1$.
Writing $X^{\Gamma}_j = \bigcup_{m\in\mathcal{I}_j} X^{\Gamma_j}_m$ for the
union over all such faces, the loss ties $u_j$ and its derivatives, on every
interior face, to the frozen root prediction $u_0$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{\Gamma}_j
= \frac{1}{|X^{\Gamma}_j|}
\sum_{m\in\mathcal{I}_j}
\sum_{(x,t)\in X^{\Gamma_j}_m}
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}
\bigl\| \partial_{x_m}^k u_j(x,t) - \partial_{x_m}^k u_0(x,t) \bigr\|^2 .
\label{eq:interface_loss}
\end{equation}
In this way every expert learns a well-posed local PDE whose boundary data on
the interior interfaces is supplied by the root, so the leaves can be trained
independently, and in parallel, while remaining mutually consistent.

\noindent When model size is not a concern, one may set
$\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{exp}} = \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{base}}$ and initialize each
expert by copying the weights of the root, so that at initialization every
expert reproduces the root prediction on its region. This warm start reduces
expert training time. The experts are trained with the same optimizer
schedule as the root (Section~\ref{sec:root_training}): 40k SSBroyden
iterations, with no Adam warm-up. Unlike the root, however, the experts use no
RAD resampling, each leaf draws a fixed, uniformly sampled point set
proportional to its volume, held fixed for the whole segment, so that every
leaf, including thin ones around a fine feature, trains on a stable,
comparable number of points regardless of its size. The loss
weights $\lambda_r$, $\lambda_{ic}$, and $\lambda_{bc}$ are kept from the root
training, and the interface weight is set to $\lambda_{\Gamma} = 1$. Examples of interface training samples for inner domain experts are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:local_expert_training}.

\subsection{Smooth Blending and Global Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:smoothstep_windows}

The trained leaf experts are combined into a single global approximation
through smooth, compactly supported window functions. Let
$\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d\times[0,T]$ denote the computational domain, and let
$\{\Omega_i\}_{i=0}^{k}$ be the set of regions obtained from the hierarchical
decision tree decomposition, where each region is an axis-aligned hypercube
\[
\Omega_i = \Bigl(\textstyle\prod_{m=1}^{d} [a_{i,m}, b_{i,m}]\Bigr) \times [a_{i,t}, b_{i,t}],
\]
i.e. a spatial box in $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\in\mathbb{R}^d$ together with a time
interval.

\noindent To smoothly blend the contributions of different experts while
ensuring compatibility with the PDE's differential order, we define compactly
supported window functions with configurable smoothness. Let $N$ denote the
smoothness order, chosen such that $N$ is at least the highest order of any
partial derivative appearing in the PDE (e.g., $N \geq 2$ for Burgers or
Allen--Cahn, $N \geq 3$ for KdV, $N \geq 4$ for KS). Since the domain
decomposition operates over all dimensions including time, no dimension
receives special treatment and the window must be sufficiently smooth for all
derivatives.

\noindent We first define the smoothstep polynomial $S_N : [0,1] \to [0,1]$,
which satisfies $S_N(0) = 0$, $S_N(1) = 1$, and
$S_N^{(k)}(0) = S_N^{(k)}(1) = 0$ for $k = 1, \ldots, N$:
\begin{align}
S_1(t) &= 3t^2 - 2t^3 \quad \text{($C^1$)} \\
S_2(t) &= 6t^5 - 15t^4 + 10t^3 \quad \text{($C^2$)} \\
S_3(t) &= 35t^4 - 84t^5 + 70t^6 - 20t^7 \quad \text{($C^3$)} \\
S_4(t) &= 126t^5 - 420t^6 + 540t^7 - 315t^8 + 70t^9 \quad \text{($C^4$)}
\end{align}

\noindent The one-sided compact ramp function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\rho_N(s) = \begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } s \leq 0 \\
S_N(s) & \text{if } 0 < s < 1 \\
1 & \text{if } s \geq 1
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent For region $\Omega_i$ with bounds $[a_{i,z}, b_{i,z}]$ along
coordinate $z$ (a spatial coordinate $x_m$, $m=1,\ldots,d$, or the time
coordinate $t$), let $\delta_{i,z} = \alpha (b_{i,z} - a_{i,z})$ denote the
collar (transition) half-width, where $\alpha > 0$ is a configurable fraction. The one dimensional window along $z$ is:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{i,z}(z) = \rho_N\!\left(\frac{z - (a_{i,z} - \delta_{i,z})}{\delta_{i,z}}\right) \cdot \rho_N\!\left(\frac{(b_{i,z} + \delta_{i,z}) - z}{\delta_{i,z}}\right)
\label{eq:window_1d}
\end{equation}

\noindent This produces a flat-top window: $\omega_{i,z}(z) = 1$ for
$z \in [a_{i,z}, b_{i,z}]$, smooth $C^N$ ramps in the collars
$[a_{i,z} - \delta_{i,z}, a_{i,z}]$ and $[b_{i,z}, b_{i,z} + \delta_{i,z}]$,
and exactly zero outside
$[a_{i,z} - \delta_{i,z}, b_{i,z} + \delta_{i,z}]$.

\noindent The region indicator function is therefore the tensor product of the
one dimensional windows over all coordinates:
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{\Omega_i}(x,t) = \omega_{i,t}(t) \prod_{m=1}^{d} \omega_{i,m}(x_m),
\qquad x = (x_1,\ldots,x_d),
\label{eq:smoothstep_windows}
\end{equation}

\noindent Given the indicators, the global solution of AToE is the
partition of unity (POU) combination over the set of leaves $\mathcal{L}$ of
the trimmed tree:
\begin{equation}
u_\theta(x,t) =
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{L}}
\tilde{\Psi}_{\Omega_j}(x,t)\, u_j(x,t),
\label{eq:global_atoe}
\end{equation}
with the leaf normalization
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\Psi}_{\Omega_i}(x,t) = \frac{\Psi_{\Omega_i}(x,t)}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{L}} \Psi_{\Omega_j}(x,t)}.
\label{eq:normalization_atoe}
\end{equation}
Once the leaf experts are spawned the root retires from the blend, it is used
only for the tree construction and the interface data, and the composition is
carried by the leaves alone. Example normalized blending weights are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:normalization_weights}.

\subsubsection{Adaptive Collar Sizing}
\label{sec:adaptive_collar}

The fixed collar above assigns every region the same relative half-width
$\delta_{i,z} = \alpha (b_{i,z} - a_{i,z})$ along every coordinate. However,
a property of decision trees in general, is that a split is placed where it reduces approximation error
the most, which is exactly where the function varies most sharply. The tree
of Section~\ref{sec:tree_construction} is no exception, so its interfaces
tend to coincide with the sharpest features of $u_0$, precisely where a
smooth blend is hardest to construct. We replace the fixed fraction $\alpha$
with a rule that sizes each collar directly from the solution, general
across PDEs and requiring no per-benchmark tuning.

\noindent A narrow collar keeps most of $\Omega_i$ under $u_i$'s own,
independently trained, local solution, the central benefit of leaf-local
training, where each expert solves an easier problem than the original PDE. This is
desirable wherever the interface itself is smooth. At an interface that
coincides with a region of high error in the root, for instance the shock in
Burgers' equation, the boundary data supplied to $u_i$ and $u_j$ by $u_0$
(Section~\ref{sec:expert_training}) inherits that error, and a narrow collar
leaves little room for the neighbouring expert to correct it through
blending. A wide collar gives the blend more room to reconcile the two
solutions there, but does so uniformly, sacrificing the easier-local-problem
benefit even where it was not needed. We therefore want $\delta$ set
adaptively, per face, rather than by a single global $\alpha$.

\noindent For a coordinate $z\in\{x_1,\ldots,x_d,t\}$, let $g_z(x,t) =
\|\partial_z u_0(x,t)\|_2$ denote the magnitude of the gradient of the root
prediction along $z$ (the $\ell_2$ norm accounts for multi-output problems),
and let $\mathrm{ref}_z(q)$ denote the $q$-quantile of $g_z$ over the whole
domain $\Omega$, for a fixed $q\in(0,1)$. For region $\Omega_i$, let
$\bar g_z(s)$ denote the mean of $g_z$ over the face of $\Omega_i$ normal to
$z$ with its $z$-coordinate held at $s$. The adaptive collar half-width on the
$a_{i,z}$ side is
\begin{equation}
\delta_{i,z}^{-} = \min\bigl\{\,\delta \ge 0 : \bar g_z(a_{i,z}-\delta) < \mathrm{ref}_z(q)\,\bigr\},
\label{eq:adaptive_collar}
\end{equation}
i.e. we push the collar outward from the interface until the mean gradient
across the bound first drops below the threshold, and symmetrically for
$\delta_{i,z}^{+}$ on the $b_{i,z}$ side. Both are clamped to
$[\sigma_{\min},\sigma_{\max}]\cdot(b_{i,z}-a_{i,z})$, where the floor $\sigma_{\min}$
keeps a minimal transition even where the threshold is met immediately, and
the cap $\sigma_{\max}$ bounds the search; if $\bar g_z$ never drops below
$\mathrm{ref}_z(q)$ within the capped window, $\delta$ is instead set to its
argmin there, so the rule is always well defined. The two per-side
half-widths $\delta_{i,z}^{-}$, $\delta_{i,z}^{+}$ replace the single
$\delta_{i,z}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:window_1d}, sizing the two ramps of
$\omega_{i,z}$ independently. The gradient is evaluated once, on the frozen
root $u_0$, so the collar geometry is fixed before expert training begins and
does not depend on the blending it defines.

\noindent This resolves the tension above directly, as at a quiet interface the
mean gradient already lies below $\mathrm{ref}_z(q)$ at, or just past, the
boundary, so $\delta$ collapses to the floor and the corresponding experts
keep nearly all of their independently trained territory. At an interface
coinciding with a sharp feature, the mean gradient stays above threshold for
longer, the collar widens, and a larger portion of the domain there is
governed by the blended composition rather than a single expert, exactly the
region the fine-tuning stage below targets with RAD resampling to reconcile.

\noindent Finally, the full composition \eqref{eq:global_atoe} is jointly fine
tuned under the global PDE loss. This last stage reconciles the independently
trained experts in the collar zones where neighboring windows overlap,
producing a single, globally consistent solution. The fine tuning stage
applies the same training scheme as the root (Section~\ref{sec:root_training}):
40k SSBroyden iterations with no Adam warm-up, and RAD resampling refreshed
every 500 iterations. Here RAD is not merely helpful but essential: after
